The Trolley Problem

A set of imaginary cases, each trading one life against five, designed to test how we actually decide what is right. (‘Trolley’ is the American word for what we in Britain call a tram — the runaway vehicle in every diagram below.) Use the buttons under each diagram to set the choice and watch what follows.

View textual sources

1.  The Switch

Philippa Foot · 1967

A runaway tram is hurtling down the track towards five people who cannot escape. You stand beside a lever. Leave it and the tram runs straight on; pull it and the tram turns onto a side track, where one person stands. Which way should the lever go?

2.  The Loop

Judith Jarvis Thomson · 1985

Again you have a lever. But the side track now loops back round to the main line, so the tram would return and kill the five anyway — unless the one large person on the loop stops it. Here the one person’s body is not an unlucky side effect; it is what saves the five. Leave the lever, or pull it?

3.  The Footbridge

Judith Jarvis Thomson · 1976

The same tram is about to kill five people. This time you are on a footbridge above the track, beside a large stranger. There is no lever. The only way to stop the tram is to push the stranger off the bridge into its path: his body would halt it, but he would die. The arithmetic is exactly that of the Loop, one body used to save five; what has changed is the means. In the Loop you pulled a lever; here you must lay your own hands on a man and push him to his death. Hold back, or push?

4.  The Transplant

After Philippa Foot and Judith Jarvis Thomson

No tram this time, but the same arithmetic. A surgeon has five patients who will die without transplants. A healthy visitor arrives for a check-up, and his organs would save all five. The surgeon’s choice is the lever here. Refuse, or operate?

5.  The Puzzle

In every case one life is traded for five, yet most people pull the lever in the Switch but refuse in the Footbridge and the Transplant. One way of thinking counts only the outcome: five deaths are worse than one, so saving the greater number is always right. Another way holds that some actions — using a person’s body as a tool, or deliberately killing the innocent — are wrong in themselves, whatever the numbers. The trolley problem is built to make these two ways of thinking collide.

For discussion. Why does pulling the lever feel different from pushing the stranger off the bridge, when both trade one life for five? Is there a real moral difference between them, or only a difference in how they feel?
